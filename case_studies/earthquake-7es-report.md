## Element 1: INPUT — Resources, Signals, and Energy Entering the System

The earthquake system receives inputs from multiple mechanistically distinct subsystems.

**Subsystem 1.1 — Tectonic Stress (Plate-Boundary Loading):**
The primary input is the slow, continuous accumulation of elastic strain energy driven by the relative motion of lithospheric plates. This stress originates from forces such as ridge push (where new lithosphere at mid-ocean ridges pushes plates apart) and slab pull (where dense subducting lithosphere pulls the trailing plate into the mantle). This is a sustained, long-duration input operating on timescales of decades to millennia.

**Subsystem 1.2 — Gravitational Loading:**
Gravitational forces represent a distinct, non-tectonic input source. Gravitational stress is predominantly vertical and produces lateral variations, particularly near the Earth's surface. Gravitational loading contributes to confining pressure at depth and influences fault behavior independently of horizontal tectonic forces.

**Subsystem 1.3 — Thermal Energy (Mantle Convection):**
Heat from the Earth's interior drives mantle convection, which is the ultimate engine of plate motion. Thermal energy input governs the rate and pattern of convective flow, and therefore the rate at which tectonic stress is supplied to fault systems. This input operates on geological timescales and represents a deep, thermodynamic energy source distinct from the mechanical stress at fault planes.

**Subsystem 1.4 — Fluid Pressure (Pore Pressure):**
Fluids within fault zones (groundwater, metamorphic fluids, magmatic volatiles) exert pore pressure on fault surfaces. Elevated pore pressure reduces the effective normal stress on a fault, lowering its frictional resistance and bringing it closer to failure. This input is hydrogeological in nature and operates through a mechanism fundamentally different from solid-state tectonic loading. It can be altered by human activity (e.g., wastewater injection), introducing an anthropogenic input channel.

**Subsystem 1.5 — Coulomb Stress Transfer from Adjacent Events:**
Stress changes imparted by prior earthquakes on neighboring or distant faults constitute a distinct input. When one fault ruptures, it redistributes stress in the surrounding crust, increasing Coulomb stress on some faults (promoting failure) and decreasing it on others (inhibiting failure). This input is episodic, exogenous, and mechanistically distinct from steady-state tectonic loading.

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Input is not a single unified function. At least five mechanistically distinct input subsystems contribute to earthquake initiation, each operating through different physical mechanisms (elastic strain, gravity, thermal convection, fluid pressure, and static/dynamic stress transfer).

## Element 2: OUTPUT — Products, Actions, and Signals Generated by the System

The earthquake system generates outputs through multiple distinct channels.

**Subsystem 2.1 — Body Waves (P-Waves and S-Waves):**
Upon fault rupture, the sudden release of elastic strain energy radiates outward through the Earth's interior as body waves. These divide into two mechanistically distinct types: P-waves (primary/compressional waves), which are longitudinal and travel through solids, liquids, and gases at approximately 6–8 km/s in crustal rock; and S-waves (secondary/shear waves), which are transverse, travel only through solids at approximately 3.5–4.5 km/s, and cannot penetrate the liquid outer core. The inability of S-waves to pass through liquid provided the first evidence of Earth's molten outer core.

**Subsystem 2.2 — Surface Waves (Love Waves and Rayleigh Waves):**
When body waves reach the Earth's surface, they generate surface waves that propagate along the boundary. Love waves produce horizontal shearing motion; Rayleigh waves produce elliptical, rolling motion. Surface waves travel more slowly than body waves but carry larger amplitudes and longer durations, making them the primary cause of structural damage.

**Subsystem 2.3 — Permanent Ground Deformation:**
Fault slip produces measurable, permanent displacement of the Earth's surface. This includes vertical uplift or subsidence and horizontal offset. Geodetic measurements (GPS, InSAR) can detect co-seismic deformation ranging from millimeters to meters. This output is a static, geometric transformation, distinct from the transient wave outputs.

**Subsystem 2.4 — Tsunami Generation:**
When an earthquake ruptures a fault beneath the ocean (particularly thrust faults at subduction zones), the vertical displacement of the seafloor displaces the overlying water column, generating tsunami waves. Earthquakes of approximately magnitude 6.5–7.0 or greater with predominantly vertical displacement are generally required for significant tsunami generation. This output is a hydrodynamic phenomenon mediated by the ocean, operating through entirely different physics than seismic wave propagation through rock.

**Subsystem 2.5 — Thermal Output (Frictional Heat):**
The rupture process generates significant frictional heat along the fault surface. In extreme cases, flash heating can melt rock, producing pseudotachylyte (fault glass). This thermal output is a direct conversion of mechanical energy to heat, distinct from wave radiation.

**Subsystem 2.6 — Secondary Environmental Effects:**
Earthquakes trigger landslides, soil liquefaction, surface rupture, and fires (from ruptured gas lines in built environments). These are cascading outputs that become inputs to other systems (ecological, infrastructural, social).

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Output is not a single unified function. At least six distinct output channels are identifiable, each operating through different physical mechanisms (compressional wave propagation, shear wave propagation, surface wave interference, hydrodynamic displacement, frictional heat conversion, and cascading geomorphological/infrastructural effects).

## Element 3: PROCESSING — Transformation of Inputs into Outputs

The earthquake system processes inputs through multiple sequential and parallel mechanisms.

**Subsystem 3.1 — Elastic Strain Accumulation:**
Tectonic stress input is stored as elastic strain energy in the rock surrounding a locked fault. The rocks deform elastically over time, behaving as an energy storage medium. This is a slow, continuous process operating over the interseismic period (years to centuries). The elastic rebound theory, formulated by H.F. Reid following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, describes this accumulation and subsequent release as the core mechanism of earthquake generation.

**Subsystem 3.2 — Rupture Nucleation:**
When accumulated stress exceeds the frictional strength of the fault at a localized point (often an asperity — a strongly locked patch), rupture initiates. This nucleation phase involves a transition from quasi-static strain to dynamic fracture. It is governed by rate-and-state friction laws and represents a qualitatively different processing regime from strain accumulation: a phase transition from stability to instability.

**Subsystem 3.3 — Rupture Propagation:**
Once nucleated, the rupture front propagates along the fault plane, typically at speeds below (but sometimes exceeding) the local shear wave velocity. The propagation process involves dynamic fracture mechanics, stress intensity factors at the crack tip, and interactions with fault geometry (bends, stepovers, barriers). Barriers — regions of the fault strong enough to resist rupture — can arrest propagation and thereby determine the earthquake's final magnitude.

**Subsystem 3.4 — Energy Partitioning:**
The total released energy is partitioned among multiple output channels: radiated seismic energy (waves), frictional heat, surface energy (creation of new fracture surfaces), and permanent deformation. This partitioning process is governed by the rupture dynamics and fault properties, and the ratios vary between events. Only a fraction of the total energy (often less than 10%) is radiated as seismic waves; most is dissipated as heat.

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Processing is not a single unified transformation. At least four distinct processing subsystems operate in sequence (accumulation → nucleation → propagation → partitioning), each governed by different physical laws and operating on different timescales (centuries for accumulation; seconds for propagation).

## Element 4: CONTROLS — Proactive Constraints Guiding System Behavior

Per the 7ES_REF_v1.3 definition, controls are proactive constraints embedded in the system's design that guide behavior in advance, as distinguished from reactive feedback. The earthquake system contains multiple distinct control subsystems.

**Subsystem 4.1 — Fundamental Physical Laws:**
Conservation of energy, conservation of momentum, and thermodynamic principles constrain all earthquake processes. These are universal, immutable controls that set absolute boundaries on system behavior. They are not responsive to outcomes; they are prior constraints inherent to the physical domain.

**Subsystem 4.2 — Material Properties (Rock Strength and Composition):**
The lithology (mineral composition, grain structure, metamorphic grade) of the rocks surrounding and composing the fault zone determines elastic moduli, shear strength, and ductile-brittle transition depth. These material properties are embedded constraints that determine how much strain can accumulate and at what threshold rupture occurs. They are proactive in the sense that they exist prior to and independent of any particular earthquake.

**Subsystem 4.3 — Fault Geometry and Orientation:**
The three-dimensional geometry of the fault system — including dip angle, strike, segmentation, bends, stepovers, and intersections — constrains how rupture nucleates, propagates, and terminates. Fault geometry is a structural control established by the tectonic history of the region, not by any individual seismic event. The three basic fault types (normal, reverse/thrust, and strike-slip) represent geometrically distinct control architectures corresponding to different tectonic stress regimes (extensional, compressional, and shearing, respectively).

**Subsystem 4.4 — Confining Pressure (Depth-Dependent Constraint):**
Lithostatic pressure increases with depth, raising the normal stress on fault surfaces and thereby increasing frictional resistance. This depth-dependent control determines the seismogenic zone — the depth range within which brittle failure (earthquakes) can occur. Below a certain depth (typically 15–20 km in continental crust), increased temperature and pressure cause rocks to deform ductilely rather than brittlely, suppressing earthquake generation.

**Subsystem 4.5 — Coulomb Failure Criterion:**
The Coulomb failure criterion (τ = c + μσₙ, where τ is shear stress, c is cohesion, μ is the coefficient of friction, and σₙ is normal stress) defines the mathematical boundary condition for fault slip. This criterion is a proactive constraint that determines the threshold at which processing transitions from strain accumulation to rupture.

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Controls comprise at least five distinct subsystems, ranging from universal physical laws to local material properties and geometric configurations. Each constrains the system through a different mechanism.

## Element 5: FEEDBACK — Existential and Operational State Information

The 7ES_REF_v1.3 introduces a critical refinement: feedback encompasses both active (dynamic) feedback loops and passive (implicit) feedback — the mere persistence of the system's structure as confirmation of viability. The earthquake system clearly exhibits both modes.

**Subsystem 5.1 — Active Feedback: Aftershock Sequences and Coulomb Stress Redistribution:**
After a mainshock, the redistribution of Coulomb stress across the surrounding fault network triggers aftershocks in zones of increased stress and suppresses seismicity in zones of decreased stress (stress shadows). This is an explicit, dynamic feedback loop: the output of the system (the mainshock) generates information (stress changes) that modifies subsequent system behavior (aftershock patterns). Research on the 2011 Tohoku earthquake demonstrated a 47–63% increase in positively stressed aftershock mechanisms compared to background seismicity rates, confirming the predictive power of Coulomb stress transfer as a feedback mechanism.

**Subsystem 5.2 — Active Feedback: Seismic Monitoring and Observational Loops:**
The earthquake generates seismic waves that are detected by global seismograph networks, processed into magnitude and location estimates, and used to update hazard models, trigger warning systems, and inform building codes. This is an anthropogenic active feedback loop in which the system's outputs are interpreted and used to modify human responses to future events.

**Subsystem 5.3 — Passive Feedback: Locked Fault Persistence (Interseismic Quiescence):**
Between earthquakes, a locked fault segment persists in a state of accumulated strain without rupturing. Per the 7ES_REF_v1.3 definition, this persistent structural stability constitutes passive feedback: the continued existence of the locked, stressed state is itself information confirming that the system's internal conditions (stress below the failure threshold) remain within viable parameters. The locked fault's endurance is the feedback.

**Subsystem 5.4 — Passive Feedback: Tectonic Plate Structural Integrity:**
The continued structural coherence of lithospheric plates — their persistence as rigid, mechanically integrated bodies — constitutes passive feedback at a larger scale. The plates' existence confirms that the thermomechanical conditions supporting rigid lithosphere remain operative. If these conditions changed (e.g., if the lithosphere became entirely ductile), the earthquake system would cease to exist.

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Feedback contains at least four distinct subsystems spanning both the active and passive modes defined in the 7ES_REF_v1.3. The passive feedback identification is particularly significant, as it demonstrates the framework's ability to identify feedback in systems where no explicit signaling loop exists — a key validation criterion for the framework's universality claim.

## Element 6: INTERFACE — Boundaries and Interaction Modalities

The earthquake system operates across multiple distinct interfaces.

**Subsystem 6.1 — Fault Plane Interface:**
The fault surface itself is the primary interface of the earthquake system. It is the boundary across which the two rock masses interact, where frictional forces mediate the exchange of stress and displacement. The fault plane's properties (roughness, gouge composition, fluid saturation) determine whether interaction takes the form of locked strain accumulation, aseismic creep, or dynamic rupture. This is the most fundamental interface in the system.

**Subsystem 6.2 — Plate Boundary Interfaces:**
At a larger scale, the three types of tectonic plate boundaries (convergent, divergent, and transform) constitute distinct interface architectures. Each boundary type mediates different interaction modalities: subduction and collision at convergent boundaries; rifting and spreading at divergent boundaries; lateral sliding at transform boundaries. These interfaces determine what types of faults (and therefore what types of earthquakes) can occur.

**Subsystem 6.3 — Lithosphere-Asthenosphere Boundary (LAB):**
The transition from the rigid lithosphere to the ductile asthenosphere defines a critical interface. This boundary controls the depth extent of seismogenic behavior (brittle failure above, ductile flow below) and mediates the transfer of convective mantle forces into lithospheric stress. The LAB is a rheological interface — it separates domains with fundamentally different mechanical response modes.

**Subsystem 6.4 — Earth Surface Interface (Geosphere–Atmosphere/Hydrosphere Boundary):**
The Earth's surface is the interface where seismic wave energy exits the solid Earth and interacts with the atmosphere (as sound), the hydrosphere (as tsunamis), and the built environment (as ground shaking affecting structures). This interface is where the earthquake system's outputs enter the human domain and where the vast majority of societal impact occurs.

**Subsystem 6.5 — Seafloor–Ocean Interface:**
For submarine earthquakes, the seafloor–ocean boundary is the specific interface through which vertical fault displacement is transmitted to the water column, initiating tsunami waves. This interface mediates a solid-to-fluid energy transfer and is functionally distinct from the land-surface interface.

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Interface is not a single boundary but a nested set of at least five distinct interfaces operating at different scales and mediating different types of physical interaction (frictional, tectonic, rheological, geomorphological, and hydrodynamic).

## Element 7: ENVIRONMENT — External Conditions, Systems, and Contexts

The earthquake system exists within and is influenced by multiple environmental contexts.

**Subsystem 7.1 — Mantle and Core (Deep Earth Thermal Environment):**
The Earth's mantle and core provide the thermal energy that drives convection and, ultimately, plate motion. This deep-Earth environment sets the rate and geometry of tectonic forcing. Changes in mantle dynamics (plume activity, convective reorganization) alter the environmental conditions under which the earthquake system operates.

**Subsystem 7.2 — Adjacent Tectonic Plates and Fault Systems:**
The behavior of neighboring plates and faults constitutes the immediate tectonic environment. The velocity, direction, and coupling of adjacent plates determine the stress regime experienced by any given fault. Rupture on one fault alters the stress environment for neighboring faults.

**Subsystem 7.3 — Hydrosphere (Oceans and Groundwater):**
The ocean provides the medium through which tsunami outputs propagate and represents an environmental resource/constraint for submarine earthquakes. Groundwater systems influence pore pressure on faults and can be altered by both natural and human activity.

**Subsystem 7.4 — Atmosphere and Climate:**
While atmospheric effects on earthquakes are minimal in direct mechanical terms, climate-driven processes (glacial loading/unloading, sea-level changes, erosion) can alter stress on faults over geological timescales. Post-glacial isostatic rebound, for example, has been associated with increased seismicity in formerly glaciated regions.

**Subsystem 7.5 — Human-Built Environment (Anthroposphere):**
Human infrastructure (cities, dams, mines, injection wells) constitutes an increasingly relevant environmental context. Induced seismicity from wastewater injection, reservoir impoundment, and mining demonstrates that anthropogenic modifications to the environment can alter earthquake system behavior. The built environment also determines the societal consequences of earthquake outputs.

**Subsystem 7.6 — Extraterrestrial Influences:**
Tidal forces from the Moon and Sun exert small but measurable periodic stress variations on faults. While the magnitude of tidal stress is far below typical earthquake stress drops, research has identified statistical correlations between tidal loading and earthquake triggering under certain conditions, representing a subtle but real environmental input.

**Multi-Subsystem Finding:** Environment encompasses at least six distinct contextual domains, ranging from the deep Earth interior to extraterrestrial gravitational influences, each affecting the earthquake system through different mechanisms and at different spatial and temporal scales.
